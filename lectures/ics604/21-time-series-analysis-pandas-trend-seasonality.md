# Lecture 21: Polynomial Regression (Cont'd.), Introduction to Time Series Analysis, and Pandas Time Series Tools

### Quick Overview
- Lecture 21 starts by closing the polynomial-regression discussion from Lecture 20. The slides say higher-degree polynomials can keep lowering RSS on the training data, but that extra flexibility can create unrealistic oscillations, fit noise, and fail to generalize. The lecture therefore recommends a train/validation split to choose polynomial degree instead of trusting training fit alone. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p1-p6]
- The main topic is an introduction to time series analysis. The lecture defines time series as data tracked over time, explains why the ordering matters, and stresses that time series usually break the independent-and-identically-distributed assumptions used in earlier lectures. It also separates two broad model families: regression-based models that use time-derived predictors, and models that use past values and past errors directly. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p7-p10]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 3-6]
- The Mauna Loa CO2 example anchors the rest of the lecture. Using `pandas`, the notebook shows how to assign a `DatetimeIndex`, slice by partial date strings, extract calendar parts, and build exploratory plots that reveal both a stable yearly seasonal pattern and a long-run upward trend. The lecture stops after the hypothesis-forming stage; the actual fitted time-series model is deferred. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p13-p25]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 12-58]

#### In Layman's Terms
- Lecture 21 says two connected things. First, a more flexible curve is not automatically a better model just because it hugs the training data more closely. Second, once time becomes part of the data, you have to study repeating patterns and long-term movement directly, because the observations are no longer just a shuffled pile of independent points. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p5-p10, p21-p25]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 3-6, 40-58]

### A. Polynomial Regression Continuation: Better Training Fit Can Still Mean a Worse Model
- The opening slides continue Lecture 20 by restating polynomial regression as a two-step process: transform `x` into higher-degree features, then fit a linear model on those transformed columns. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p1-p2]
- The rendered opening example makes that workflow concrete by showing a `20`-point subsample expanded with `PolynomialFeatures(degree=2)` and then fitted with a curved degree-2 line. That visual bridge helps explain why polynomial regression is still "linear" in the coefficients even when the picture on the page is curved. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p3-p4]
- The key warning is that picking the model with the smallest training-data RSS can push you toward higher-degree polynomials that bend too much. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p5-p6]
- The slides list the main shortcomings of higher-order polynomials: they can introduce many changes in direction, capture noise rather than signal, overfit the data, and perform poorly on unseen data. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p6]
- The recommended fix is a train/validation split to choose the polynomial degree, and the slides frame that strategy as a general machine-learning idea, not just a polynomial-regression trick. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p6]
- The companion notebook effectively starts after this transition, so the polynomial-regression wrap-up lives in the PDF rather than in notebook code for Lecture 21. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p1-p6]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 1-3]
- The shared notebook `ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb` reproduces the Lecture 21 time-series material through the exploratory stage, then continues into Lecture 22 model building. For Lecture 21, it confirms the handoff point at `### 3. Specifying and Testing a Model`. [NB2: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 1-58]

#### In Layman's Terms
- A very wiggly curve can win the training-data contest by memorizing noise. The lecture's point is that the best model is the one that will still behave well on new data, not the one that makes the prettiest fit on the old data. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p5-p6]

### B. What Makes Time Series Different from the Data Used Earlier
- The lecture defines time series as data where we follow a quantity over time and care about patterns such as growth, cycles, and fluctuations. The examples include traffic counts, travelers arriving at HNL, and temperature changes over time. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p7-p8]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 3-4]
- It says time series differ from prior datasets in two ways: observations are not independent, and observations are not necessarily identically distributed. The value at time `t` can depend on the value at time `t-1`, so order matters. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p8]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cell 4]
- The goals of time series analysis are to describe structure, identify patterns such as trend and recurring behavior, forecast future values, and assess forecast variability. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p9]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cell 5]
- Two model classes are introduced: regression-based models using time components as explanatory variables, and models based on past values and past prediction errors. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p9]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cell 5]
- The lecture limits scope to univariate time series and explicitly says this is only an introductory overview, not a full course treatment. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p7]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cell 3]

#### In Layman's Terms
- In earlier lectures you could often pretend each row was its own separate case. In time series, yesterday and today are connected, so if you shuffle the rows you destroy part of the information you were trying to learn. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p7-p9]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 3-5]

### C. Two Broad Time Series Patterns: Random Walks Versus Trend + Seasonality + Noise
- The slides contrast random walks, where apparent patterns are mostly noise, with structured series that combine trend, seasonality, and random fluctuations. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p10]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cell 6]
- For a random walk, the lecture writes `v_t = v_{t-1} + epsilon_t` and uses stock returns as the rough example. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p10]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 6-8]
- For a structured series, the lecture uses `v_t = f(t) + s(t) + epsilon_t`, where `f(t)` is the trend and `s(t)` is the seasonal function. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p10]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 6, 9-11]
- The Mauna Loa CO2 data is then introduced as a real example with all three components: monthly CO2 measurements recorded from January 1959 to December 1990 on the Big Island. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p13]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cell 12]
- The notebook makes the decomposition concrete with synthetic code and then moves to the real CO2 dataset. [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 7-13]
- The rendered synthetic plots make the contrast easy to see. One figure wanders like a random walk with no stable repeating shape, while the next sequence builds a structured series by stacking a rising trend, a sinusoidal seasonal wave, and noise into one combined signal. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p10-p13]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 7-12]

#### In Layman's Terms
- The lecture gives you two mental pictures. A random walk is mostly wandering. A structured time series has a shape you can describe, like a steady climb plus a yearly wave plus a little leftover noise. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p10, p13]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 6-12]

### D. `pandas` Time Series Basics: Building and Using a `DatetimeIndex`
- The lecture says `pandas` has strong time-series support, including alignment, subsampling, and missing-value handling. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p14]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cell 15]
- The notebook creates a monthly date index with `pd.date_range('1/1/1959', periods=len(co2_data), freq="MS")` and assigns it to the CO2 data so the DataFrame uses timestamps instead of plain integer positions. [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 15-19]
- The rendered slides show the resulting `DatetimeIndex` has length `384`, matching the monthly span from January 1959 through December 1990. Right after that conversion, the full CO2 line plot already shows the two patterns the lecture will later analyze more carefully: a steady upward baseline plus a regular yearly wiggle. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p15-p16]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 16-20]
- Once the index is `datetime64`, partial string indexing becomes very convenient: `co2_data.loc['1960']`, `co2_data.loc['1960-4']`, and `co2_data.loc['1960-9':'1961-3']`. The slides note that date-range slicing includes the end index. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p16]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 21-26]
- The lecture also shows how to pull calendar pieces from the index, such as day, day of week, month names, and formatted strings using `strftime`, then use `index.year.isin([...])` to filter selected years. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p18]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 27-39]

#### In Layman's Terms
- The main Pandas trick is simple: once the index is real dates instead of row numbers, you can ask for data using calendar language instead of manual positions. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p14-p18]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 15-39]

#### Language Bridge
- A `DatetimeIndex` is like swapping out anonymous row numbers for timestamp keys. After that, expressions like `df.loc['1960']` behave more like keyed lookups on a calendar than like numeric slicing by offsets. [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 16-26]
- Methods such as `.strftime('%b')`, `.index.year`, and `.index.dayofweek` are feature-extraction helpers. They turn one timestamp field into more specific calendar fields you can filter on, group by, or reuse in a model. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p18]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 27-39]

### E. Exploring Seasonality Before Modeling
- The lecture frames time-series modeling as a three-step process: explore the data, form a hypothesis about the components, and then write and test a model. It adds that residual noise itself is not something we can model deterministically. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p21]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 40-41]
- To look for seasonality, the notebook first plots four separate years, `1960`, `1970`, `1980`, and `1990`, and the resulting shapes suggest a roughly sinusoidal pattern with a one-year period. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p21-p22, p25]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 36, 42-43]
- The rendered four-year panel adds an important visual detail: the crest-and-trough shape stays nearly the same from decade to decade, while the whole wave sits higher in later years. That is exactly the pattern you would expect from stable seasonality plus an upward trend. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p20-p22]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 36, 42-43]
- The notebook then creates month and year columns, pivots the data so years are rows and months are columns, reorders the month columns, and uses a monthly boxplot to examine the seasonal pattern across the whole dataset. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p22, p25]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 44-49]
- Slide 22 also pauses to label the anatomy of a boxplot, which matters here because each monthly box is summarizing all Januaries, all Februaries, and so on across the full date range. That makes the month-by-month seasonality comparison much easier to read. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p22-p24]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 44-48]
- The lecture says agreement between the line plots and boxplots supports the seasonality hypothesis and suggests a stable yearly seasonal component. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p25]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cell 49]

#### In Layman's Terms
- The lecture does not jump straight to a formula. It first asks, "Do I really see the same yearly wave over and over again?" Looking at a few raw yearly traces and then a month-by-month boxplot is two different ways to answer that same question. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p21-p25]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 41-49]

### F. Exploring Trend with Yearly Summaries and Resampling
- Within the lecture's exploratory modeling stage, the notebook groups observations by year and uses boxplots to compare the distribution of CO2 values across selected years. If the medians shift upward over time, that is evidence of an upward trend. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p21]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 50-53]
- It then aggregates the monthly series to yearly values with `co2_data['co2_val'].resample('YS').sum()`, which smooths short-term fluctuations and makes the long-run movement easier to see. [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 54-56]
- The yearly-wave plots support the same conclusion from another angle: the seasonal ups and downs repeat, but the whole pattern is vertically shifted upward from `1960` to `1990`. The annual-sum scatterplot then strips away most of the monthly wobble and leaves a clean rising long-run pattern. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p20-p22]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 36, 50-56]
- The notebook says both the yearly boxplots and the annual aggregates point to the same conclusion: the CO2 series has a steady upward trend. [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cell 57]
- The lecture stops after this hypothesis-forming stage. The notebook ends with `### 3. Specifying and Testing a Model` followed by `To be continued...`, so Lecture 21 is about identifying components and preparing the data, not yet fitting the final time-series model. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p21]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 57-58]

#### In Layman's Terms
- Trend is the slow background movement. The lecture shows two ways to make it easier to see: compare whole years with boxplots, or collapse monthly data into one value per year. When both views point upward, the trend claim becomes much harder to dismiss as noise. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec21-TimeSeriesAnalysis-1.pdf p21]; [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 50-58]

#### Language Bridge
- `resample('YS')` is a time-aware grouping operation. It is the time-series version of saying "bucket these rows by year start, then summarize each bucket." [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 54-56]
- The `pivot(index='Year', columns='Month', values='co2_val')` step is a reshape that turns one long table into a year-by-month grid, which is exactly the kind of structure a month-by-month boxplot needs. [NB1: ics604-21_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 44-48]
