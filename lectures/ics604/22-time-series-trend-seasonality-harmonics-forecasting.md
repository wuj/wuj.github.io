# Lecture 22: Time Series Trend Modeling, Seasonality, Harmonics, and Forecasting

### Quick Overview
- Lecture 22 starts with a short recap of the earlier trend evidence: year-by-year boxplots shift upward over time, and annual `resample('YS').sum()` values also rise steadily. After that reminder, the lecture moves into actual model building and recommends two setup steps before fitting functions: shift the series if helpful so it starts near the origin, and replace date labels with a numeric time axis such as "months since the first observation." It also clarifies what counts as noise and why smoothing is often better than collapsing monthly data into yearly aggregates. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p2-p10]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 53-64]
- For the Mauna Loa CO2 series, the lecture uses a centered sliding window of size `12` to smooth the data, compares alternate window sizes, and argues that a power law is a better trend model than an exponential curve because it is easier to tune and less sensitive to small parameter changes. The notebook then fits that power law with `scipy.optimize.curve_fit()` and checks the fit visually. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p10-p16]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 65-77]
- After fitting the trend, the lecture subtracts it, models the remaining yearly cycle with a sine curve, notices leftover residual seasonality, and adds a second harmonic with a `6`-month period to improve the fit. It closes by extending the final model `36` months forward to make a simple forecast. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p17-p28]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 78-102]

#### In Layman's Terms
- Lecture 22 shows how to turn "this series seems to rise and wiggle every year" into a concrete formula. First smooth the data enough to see the long-term rise, then remove that rise so the yearly pattern stands out, and finally add one more faster wave when a single yearly wave is not enough. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p10-p28]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 65-102]

### A. From Exploration to Modeling: Shift the Series, Encode Time Numerically, and Define Noise
- The deck opens by recalling the three-stage workflow from the previous lecture: explore the trend and seasonality, form a hypothesis about the components, and then write and test a model. Lecture 22 picks up at that third stage. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p2]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cell 58]
- Before giving new modeling advice, the slides briefly revisit the earlier evidence for trend. The yearly boxplots move upward from earlier years to later years, and the annual values produced by `resample('YS').sum()` also climb steadily. Together, those visuals justify treating long-run growth as a real component that the model must capture. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p3-p5]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 53-57]
- The lecture's first practical tip is to shift the data so it passes near the origin. The notebook does this by subtracting the first CO2 observation and storing the result in `normalized_col`. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p6]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 58-59]
- The second tip is to turn the x-axis into a plain numeric variable, such as the number of months since the first observation. That makes it easier to plug time into functions than working directly with date strings. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p7]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 58-59]
- The lecture defines time-series noise as an additive signal with a constant mean, constant standard deviation, and values that are independent across time. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p9]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cell 62]
- It also makes an important modeling point: if the immediate goal is to study the trend, then seasonality can temporarily be treated as noise. But simply averaging by year throws away too many monthly points, so the lecture prefers smoothing methods that keep more of the original structure. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p9-p10]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 63-64]

#### In Layman's Terms
- The lecture first reminds you why a trend component belongs in the model at all, then it cleans up the workspace before doing algebra. Start the series at zero if that helps, replace calendar labels with month counts, and decide which part of the signal you care about right now. Everything else is temporary clutter you want to reduce, not necessarily explain yet. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p3-p10]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 53-64]

#### Language Bridge
- `normalized_col.index = range(0, len(normalized_col))` is the notebook's way to replace date keys with a simple integer timeline. In programming terms, it is like adding an `elapsedMonths` field so the model can use ordinary numeric input. [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cell 59]
- The lecture's use of "noise" is goal-dependent. When the task is trend modeling, a seasonal swing can be treated as nuisance variation even though it becomes the main target in the next stage. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p9]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 62-63]

### B. Smoothing the Series and Choosing a Stable Trend Model
- To reduce noise without collapsing the data into a tiny yearly series, the lecture uses a sliding window smoother. In the notebook, this is `normalized_col['co2_val'].rolling(window=12, center=True).mean()`, which averages a `12`-month neighborhood around each point. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p10, p13]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cell 65]
- The slides stress that sliding-window smoothing is not the same as yearly aggregation. A `3`-year monthly series still leaves many smoothed monthly points, while yearly aggregation would shrink those data into only `3` summary values. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p10]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 64-65]
- The lecture then asks what happens when the window size changes. A larger window smooths more aggressively and can hide useful detail, while a smaller window keeps more short-term variation but leaves more noise. The notebook compares windows `8`, `12`, `17`, and `24` to make that tradeoff visible. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p11-p13]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 66-69]
- The rendered comparison plots make that tradeoff concrete. The `8`-month window still shows a clear sawtooth seasonal pattern, while the `24`-month window smooths much more heavily and keeps mainly the long upward rise. The `12`- and `17`-month windows sit between those two extremes. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p11-p12]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 66-68]
- Another detail is the edge effect. With a centered window of size `12`, the first `6` and last `5` observations cannot be smoothed because there are not enough neighbors on both sides. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p13]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cell 69]
- For the trend itself, the lecture tests candidate parent functions and concludes that a power law, `f(x) = c0 + c * x^m`, is more appropriate than an exponential model for this dataset. The reason is not only shape, but also stability: the slides say small parameter changes in the exponential can create very large curve changes. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p8, p13-p15]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 60-72]
- The notebook first plots several hand-tuned power-law candidates, then uses `curve_fit()` from `scipy.optimize` to estimate the parameters automatically. The lecture notes that `curve_fit()` returns both optimal parameters and an estimated covariance matrix, but it chooses to rely mainly on visual validation of the fitted curve. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p15-p16]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 73-77]

#### In Layman's Terms
- Smoothing gives you a cleaner picture of the slow rise, and the window size controls how aggressive that cleanup is. After that, the lecture tries curve shapes and settles on the one that is easier to control and less fragile when you nudge the parameters. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p10-p16]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 65-77]

#### Language Bridge
- `rolling(window=12, center=True).mean()` is a local recomputation rule. It does not permanently group rows into larger buckets. Instead, it recalculates each point from its nearby neighbors. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p10]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 64-65]
- `curve_fit(func_powerlaw, x_axis[6:-5], y_axis[6:-5], maxfev=2000)` is a parameter search over the power-law template. The slice `x_axis[6:-5]` matches the part of the smoothed series that actually exists after the window-based edge losses. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p13-p16]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 69, 75-76]

### C. Subtracting the Trend and Building the First Seasonal Model
- Once the power-law trend is fitted, the lecture removes it from the original shifted series. This detrending step isolates the repeating part of the signal so the seasonal pattern is easier to study. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p17-p19]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 78-80]
- The lecture models the periodic part with a sine function, `y = a * sin(b * x)`, where `a` controls amplitude and `b` controls frequency, and therefore the period. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p19]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cell 81]
- Because the CO2 cycle is yearly and the data are monthly, the lecture sets the period to `12` months by using `sin(2 * pi * x / 12)`. The deck explains this scaling in terms of the sine function's natural period `2*pi`. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p20]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 83-84]
- From visual inspection, the notebook chooses an amplitude of about `3`, giving the first seasonal approximation `3 * sin(2 * pi * x / 12)`. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p21]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 85-86]
- The lecture then combines the fitted trend and the yearly sine wave into one model for the data, while explicitly setting aside the remaining random error term. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p22]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 87-90]

#### In Layman's Terms
- After smoothing helped reveal the trend, the lecture flips the problem around. It subtracts that long-term rise so the leftover series looks mostly like a repeating yearly wave, then writes down a sine formula that matches that wave's timing and height. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p17-p22]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 78-90]

### D. Residual Seasonality and Why Higher Harmonics Help
- The first combined model already fits fairly well, and the lecture says the residuals are relatively small, around `[-2, 2]`. But when the notebook zooms into a shorter region, a repeating yearly structure is still visible in the residual plot. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p23-p24]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 91-93]
- The explanation is that one sine wave is too simple to represent the full seasonal shape. To refine the model, the lecture introduces higher harmonics, where the frequency `f_n` is an integer multiple of the fundamental frequency `f_1`. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p24-p26]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 93-95]
- The harmonics figure on slide 25 adds a visual intuition that the text extractor misses. It shows a complicated periodic curve being approximated by a large fundamental wave plus smaller, faster waves. That is the same logic used here when the lecture adds another sine term instead of throwing away the whole seasonal model. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p25]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 94-96]
- For the second harmonic, the period is cut in half from `12` months to `6` months. The notebook adds a second sine term, `0.75 * sin(2 * pi * x / 6)`, on top of the original `12`-month term. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p26-p27]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 96-97]
- The final model is therefore the sum of three parts: a power-law trend, a `12`-month seasonal sine wave, and a `6`-month second harmonic. The lecture presents this version as a much better overall approximation to the observed CO2 series. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p27]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 96-100]

#### In Layman's Terms
- One yearly wave gets the big seasonal motion right, but it misses some finer bends. The second harmonic is just a smaller, faster wave added on top so the seasonal shape stops looking too simple. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p23-p27]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 91-100]

#### Language Bridge
- A harmonic term is just another sine expression added to the model. In code terms, it is not a new algorithm. It is one more component in the same `fx(x)` function. [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 96-100]
- The move from one sine term to two sine terms is the same modeling pattern used throughout the course: keep the basic structure, then add only the extra term needed to explain the leftover pattern. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p24-p27]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 91-100]

### E. Forecasting by Extending the Time Axis
- The lecture closes by predicting `3` future years, which is `36` months. The notebook does this by extending `x_axis` beyond the observed data and evaluating the final model on that larger range. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p28]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 101-102]
- The rendered forecast slide shows the right-hand tail continuing the same upward rise and repeating yearly cycle into the new `36`-month region. Because the forecast uses only the modeled trend and seasonal terms, that future curve stays smooth instead of trying to imitate the random month-to-month bumps. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p28]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 101-102]

#### In Layman's Terms
- The forecasting step is mechanically simple: keep the same formula and feed it later month numbers. The result is a clean projection of the modeled rise and seasonal cycles, not a claim that the random bumps can also be predicted. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec22-TimeSeriesAnalysis-2.pdf p28]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 101-102]
