# Lecture 23: Residual Autocorrelation and Exponential Smoothing

### Quick Overview
- Lecture 23 begins by checking whether the regression-based time-series model from the previous lecture really removed the main structure from the CO2 data. The test is residual autocorrelation: if the residuals still correlate with lagged copies of themselves, then the model is still missing pattern. The slides and the appended shared notebook section both show especially strong leftover correlation at lag `12`, which points to remaining yearly seasonality, and then confirm the same pattern with `statsmodels` ACF plots. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p2-p7]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 103-108]
- The lecture then shifts to forecasting ideas for noisy series. It starts with random walks, where the best short-term baseline is the previous value, and then moves to sliding-window averages that smooth away short-lived spikes by averaging several recent observations instead of trusting only the last one. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p8-p18]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cells 3-22]
- After reviewing the limits of ordinary moving averages and Gaussian-weighted windows, the lecture introduces exponential smoothing. The main idea is to keep all past observations, but give smaller and smaller weight to older ones. The notebook and deck then cover single exponential smoothing, `pandas.Series.ewm()`, the effect of the smoothing parameter `alpha`, and double exponential smoothing for data with trend. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p19-p27]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cells 21-37]

#### In Layman's Terms
- Lecture 23 asks two practical questions. First, "Did our old model really remove the main pattern?" Second, "If the data are noisy, how should we smooth them before predicting the next point?" The answer first uses residual autocorrelation to check the old model, then moves from simple averages to a better idea that remembers all past data but trusts recent values more. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p3-p27]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 103-108]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cells 3-37]

### A. Residual Autocorrelation Is a Check for Leftover Structure
- The lecture opens with a model-checking step, not a new forecasting method. After fitting trend and seasonality, we should inspect the residuals. Ideally they should look like white noise, meaning no visible pattern, roughly constant variance, and no correlation across time. If residuals still correlate with their past values, the model did not finish the job. The shared notebook now mirrors that opening setup in its appended residual-analysis section. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p2-p3]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 103-106]
- Autocorrelation is defined here as the relationship between a series and lagged copies of itself. For each lag `k`, the lecture shifts the residual series by `k` time steps and computes the correlation between the original residuals and the lagged residuals. The appended shared notebook follows the same recipe through lag `12`. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p3-p4]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 103-104]
- The rendered code slide and the appended shared notebook section both build lagged residual columns and check their correlations. For the lecture's lag-12 example, the slide shows a correlation of about `0.896`, while the notebook reproduces the same lagged-scatter workflow and then adds `acf()` as a longer sweep through lag `36`. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p4-p6]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 104-107]
- The next rendered slides make the diagnosis visual. Several lag scatter plots look stretched rather than round, and the stem plot shows a very large spike at lag `12`, near `0.9`. Page `7` then repeats the message with `plot_acf()`, where seasonal spikes stand above the confidence band. Together, the slides and notebook say the residuals are not purely random and the yearly cycle is still leaking through the earlier model. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p5-p7]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 105-108]

#### In Layman's Terms
- Residual analysis is like checking whether you really cleaned a dirty surface. If you still see a repeating pattern in the leftovers, then some of the original pattern is still there. In this lecture, the big lag-12 spike says the yearly cycle was not fully removed. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p5-p7]; [NB1: ics604-21-22_time_series_regression_based.ipynb cells 106-108]

### B. Random Walks, Naive Forecasts, and Why Sliding Windows Help
- The lecture restarts forecasting from a simple case: a random walk, `v_t = v_{t-1} + epsilon_t`, where the change from one step to the next is just white noise. Since the noise cannot be predicted directly, the lecture uses the previous observed value as the baseline forecast. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p8-p9]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cells 3-6]
- This baseline is the lecture's "naive forecast." It is important because it gives a benchmark that more advanced methods should beat. The notebook makes the same point and shows the overlap between `v_t` and `v_{t-1}` visually on a simulated random walk. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p9]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cells 4-6]
- The lecture then explains why a one-point forecast can be too fragile when the series contains temporary spikes from short-lived noise sources. Examples given in the deck include finance shocks and sensor interference. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p10-p11]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cells 7-9]
- To reduce that problem, the lecture proposes a sliding-window forecast that averages the most recent `n` values: `v_(t+1) = (1/n) * sum(v_(t-i))`. This is presented as a way to smooth out temporary spikes before predicting the next point. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p12]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cells 9-11]

#### In Layman's Terms
- If the latest point might be a weird one-off spike, then using only that point is risky. Averaging the recent past gives a calmer forecast that is less easy to fool. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p10-p12]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cells 7-11]

### C. Moving Averages Smooth Noise, but Window Design Creates Tradeoffs
- The lecture ties the new smoothing idea back to Lecture 22 by reminding us that rolling averages were already used to smooth the CO2 series before fitting a trend model. The notebook repeats that connection explicitly. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p13]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cell 12]
- Two moving-average forms are shown. A centered window of size `2k + 1` uses past and future neighbors around `x_i`, while a trailing window of size `k` uses only the current point and past values. The lecture uses this contrast to separate general smoothing from forecasting, where future values are not available. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p14]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cells 13-15]
- The rendered comparison slide on page `15` makes the edge issue obvious. The centered rolling mean leaves gaps at both ends because it needs neighbors on both sides, while the trailing rolling mean can keep producing values near the right edge because it only looks backward. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p15]
- Window size controls the basic tradeoff. Larger windows smooth more aggressively and damp spikes better, but they also create more lag and can flatten real changes. Smaller windows respond faster, but they can track noise too closely. The lecture shows both sides of this tradeoff before moving on. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p16-p18]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cells 16-20]
- The lecture next considers weighted moving averages. Instead of giving every value the same contribution, we can weight points differently, such as giving more weight to values near the center of the window. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p18]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cell 20]
- A Gaussian weighting scheme is then introduced as a more sophisticated version, where points with values close to the center value get larger weights. But the lecture also lists the drawbacks: the range of values varies by window, `sigma` may need to change from window to window, the method is computationally expensive, edge effects remain, and it still does not directly model trend or seasonality. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p19]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cells 21-22]

#### In Layman's Terms
- A moving average helps, but it forces you to choose what kind of mistake you would rather make. A big window reacts slowly. A small window reacts quickly but can chase noise. Fancy weights help some, but they still do not solve the whole forecasting problem. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p16-p19]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cells 16-22]

#### Language Bridge
- `random_ts['val'].rolling(window=5, center=True).mean()` and `random_ts['val'].rolling(window=5).mean()` use the same basic operator with different rules. In programming terms, the centered version needs look-ahead data, while the trailing version is causal and can be used in a real forecast pipeline. [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cells 14-15]

### D. Exponential Smoothing Keeps All Past Data but Decays Older Influence
- Exponential smoothing is introduced as the answer to the weaknesses of fixed windows and custom local weights. Instead of throwing away old observations once they fall outside a window, the method keeps all past values and lets their influence decay as they get older. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p20]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cell 23]
- The rendered page `20` makes the idea visual by drawing arrows from many earlier observations into the current smoothed value, with the older contributions implicitly shrinking. The same slide also includes the classification table for the three main forms of exponential smoothing. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p20]
- The lecture distinguishes the forms by signal type: single exponential smoothing for data with neither trend nor seasonality, double exponential smoothing for trend without seasonality, and triple exponential smoothing for data with both trend and seasonality. The notebook states this mapping directly, while also ending the lecture with triple smoothing postponed to a later continuation. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p20]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cells 24, 37]

#### In Layman's Terms
- The big change is this: a moving average has a hard memory cutoff, but exponential smoothing has a fading memory. Old values still matter, just less and less. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p20]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cells 23-24]

### E. Single Exponential Smoothing Updates the Forecast by Blending the New Value with the Old Estimate
- Single exponential smoothing is defined recursively as `s_t = alpha * v_t + (1 - alpha) * s_(t-1)`, with `0 <= alpha <= 1`. The lecture explains the extremes clearly: `alpha = 0` means fully keeping the previous smoothed value, while `alpha = 1` means fully copying the current observation. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p21]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cell 25]
- The lecture then expands the recursion to show why the method is called exponential smoothing. Every past observation contributes to the current smoothed value, but the weights follow a geometric decay based on powers of `(1 - alpha)`. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p21-p22]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cells 26-29]
- Once the series has been smoothed, the lecture uses the current smoothed value as the next forecast: `F_(t+1) = s_t`. The rendered page `23` and the notebook both reframe the same update as an error-correction rule: `F_(t+1) = F_t + alpha * E`, where `E` is the latest forecasting error. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p23]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cell 30]
- The lecture then shows the direct Pandas implementation. `Series.ewm(alpha=0.5).mean()` computes the exponentially weighted mean, and the rendered page `24` compares that smoothed curve to an ordinary rolling window on the same data. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p24]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cells 31-32]
- Parameter `alpha` controls how fast the memory fades. The rendered page `25` shows that a large `alpha`, such as `0.8`, puts most weight on the newest point and drops older weights quickly, while a small `alpha`, such as `0.2`, keeps older points influential for much longer. The notebook then plots the resulting smoothed series for both settings. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p25]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cells 27-28, 33-34]

#### In Layman's Terms
- Single exponential smoothing is a running compromise between "believe the newest point" and "trust the existing trend of the smoothed line." `alpha` is the dial that controls which side wins more often. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p21-p25]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cells 25-34]

#### Language Bridge
- `Series.ewm()` is like creating a rolling-style object, but with exponential weights instead of a fixed window. Calling `.mean()` then materializes the smoothed series. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p24]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cell 31]
- The error-update form, `F_(t+1) = F_t + alpha * E`, is the same control idea used in many iterative algorithms: start from the old estimate, then move only part of the way toward the new correction. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p23]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cell 30]

### F. Double Exponential Smoothing Adds an Explicit Trend Estimate
- The lecture warns that single exponential smoothing is not enough when the data have trend. Double exponential smoothing fixes that by introducing a second recursively updated quantity, `r_t`, to represent the smoothed trend. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p26]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cell 35]
- The trend update is `r_t = beta * (s_t - s_(t-1)) + (1 - beta) * r_(t-1)`. The deck explains this as an exponentially weighted average of recent changes in the smoothed signal, with `beta` playing the same role for trend that `alpha` plays for level. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p26]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cell 35]
- The level update is then modified to include the previous trend term: `s_t = alpha * v_t + (1 - alpha) * (s_(t-1) + r_(t-1))`. The lecture explicitly says the assumption at this point is trend without seasonality. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p26-p27]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cells 35-36]
- Forecasting becomes `F_(t+1) = s_t + r_t`, which means the next prediction is the current smoothed level plus the current smoothed trend. The lecture stops here and leaves triple exponential smoothing for the next continuation. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p27]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cells 36-37]

#### In Layman's Terms
- Single smoothing tracks the current level. Double smoothing tracks both the level and the direction of travel. That extra trend term is what lets the forecast move upward or downward instead of staying flat. [PDF: ics604-S26-lec23-ExponentialSmoothing.ppdf.pdf p26-p27]; [NB2: ics604-23_exponential_smoothing.ipynb cells 35-37]
